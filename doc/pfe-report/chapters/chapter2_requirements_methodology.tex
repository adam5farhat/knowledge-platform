This chapter specifies the requirements and delivery methodology of the Knowledge
Platform. It begins with a functional analysis of the host organisation, the
system actors, and the global use-case model that bounds the platform's scope.
It then formulates the functional and non-functional requirements, including the
hybrid access-control model and the performance indicators used to make those
requirements measurable. UML modelling supports this analysis through the global
use-case view of the system. A comparison of software development methodologies
then justifies the choice of Agile Scrum. The chapter finally presents the selected
methodology, the five-sprint delivery plan with detailed backlogs, and the
development and modelling tools adopted for the project. The sections that follow
develop these elements in that order.

\section{Analysis of the Existing Situation and Usage Context}

\subsection{Host Organisation Context}

\textbf{\CompanyName} is a technology company whose teams produce and consume
large volumes of internal documentation: technical specifications, onboarding
guides, project reports, API references, and operational procedures. Prior to this
project, knowledge was scattered across disconnected tools such as shared drives,
email threads, and informal messaging channels, with no unified governance model and
no mechanism for intelligent retrieval.

Several pain points stood out when the existing situation was analysed.
Documents lived in multiple systems with no single point of access, so a
comprehensive search was impossible. There was no systematic control over which
teams or roles could reach which documents, which tended to produce either
over-sharing or information silos. Retrieval was limited to filename and keyword
matching and returned documents rather than answers. New employees had no reliable
way to find answers to operational questions and therefore interrupted experienced
colleagues repeatedly. When information found through search proved incorrect or
outdated, there was no way to signal that fact or to stop the same error from
recurring.

\subsection{Access Control Model}
\label{subsec:access-control-model}

Building on the RBAC concepts introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:state-of-the-art},
the platform enforces a \textbf{hybrid authorization model} at three complementary
layers. The subsections below explain how those foundations are applied to the
requirements already defined.

Under \textbf{Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)}, every user holds one platform role
(\texttt{ADMIN}, \texttt{MANAGER}, or \texttt{EMPLOYEE}) that determines
coarse-grained capabilities. Administrators manage users, departments, and platform
configuration (FR-27, FR-28, FR-30 to FR-32, FR-34 to FR-36); managers reach
department-scoped dashboards and announcements (FR-26, FR-23); employees use the
document library, search, and AI assistant within their permitted scope.

\textbf{Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)} further constrains permissions by
attributes such as department membership and document visibility
(\texttt{ALL}, \texttt{DEPARTMENT}, \texttt{PRIVATE}). In semantic search and Ask,
those predicates are applied as query-time SQL filters on both dense (pgvector)
and sparse (full-text) retrieval---not after ranking or during answer
generation---so unauthorised passages never enter fusion, reranking, or the
generative context (FR-08, FR-17, FR-18).
Subsection~\ref{subsubsec:retrieval-security} details this enforcement point in
the retrieval pipeline.

\textbf{Per-user feature restriction flags} let administrators disable specific
capabilities for individual users (for example, blocking document-library access or
AI queries) via middleware that intercepts requests before business logic runs
(FR-06).

Taken together, the three layers give fine-grained control over platform
capabilities while remaining practical to scale across authentication, document
management, search, and AI workflows.

\section{Comparison of Software Development Methodologies}
\label{sec:methodology-comparison}

Selecting a software process model is a strategic decision: it shapes how
requirements evolve, how risk is contained, and how frequently stakeholders can
inspect working software. Before adopting a delivery approach for the Knowledge
Platform, four representative methodologies were compared against the criteria
that matter most for this internship: flexibility, client feedback, risk
management, adaptability, suitability to an AI-enabled product, and support for
iterative development. Table~\ref{tab:methodology-comparison} summarises that
comparison.

Waterfall remains appropriate when requirements are stable and the problem is well
understood, but it offers little room to revise scope once coding has begun. For a
platform that combines document ingest, hybrid retrieval, and RAG-based answering,
those assumptions do not hold: retrieval quality, prompt behaviour, and
department-scoped access rules typically become clearer only after working slices
are exercised. The V-Model strengthens verification and validation discipline, yet
it inherits the same sequential rigidity and therefore remains a weak match when
stakeholder feedback and AI evaluation must reshape priorities mid-project.

The Spiral model addresses risk more explicitly by repeating analysis, evaluation,
and development cycles. That risk focus is valuable for an AI-enabled system, but
the model is comparatively heavy for a single-engineer internship with a fixed
calendar: each spiral still requires substantial planning overhead, and delivery
cadence is less standardised than a sprint-based rhythm.

Agile Scrum combines short iterative increments with continuous stakeholder
feedback, explicit backlog prioritisation, and a lightweight ceremony set that a
small team can sustain. Flexibility and adaptability are built into the sprint
cycle; risk is reduced by delivering testable vertical slices early; and AI-specific
work---such as tuning hybrid retrieval or reviewing cited answers---can be
scheduled as backlog items without freezing the overall plan. Those properties
align directly with the Knowledge Platform's uncertain technical surface and with
the need for regular industrial and academic review during the internship.

On that basis, Scrum was selected as the delivery methodology for this project.
The following section details how the framework was applied: roles, artefacts,
sprint structure, and the five-sprint plan that organised the work.

\section{Selected Methodology}

\subsection{Agile Scrum Framework}

The project used the \textbf{Agile Scrum} methodology for its iterative structure,
its focus on a working deliverable at the end of each sprint, and its ability to
absorb changing requirements. That mix suits a complex full-stack product built by
a single engineer over a bounded internship period.

\subsubsection{Scrum Roles}

\textbf{\IndustrialSupervisor{}} (\CompanyName) acted as \textbf{Product Owner},
validating feature priorities and acceptance criteria. \StudentName served as both
\textbf{Scrum Master} and developer, owning sprint planning, execution, daily
self-review, and retrospective. \textbf{\AcademicSupervisor{}} (ESPRIM) provided
methodological and scientific guidance at sprint boundaries as Academic Advisor.

\subsubsection{Scrum Artefacts}

The \textbf{Product Backlog} holds the full set of functional and non-functional
requirements (FR-01 to FR-36, NFR-01 to NFR-06), ordered by business value and
technical dependency. Each \textbf{Sprint Backlog} is the subset of those items
committed for a given sprint, broken into implementation tasks. An
\textbf{Increment} is a working, tested vertical slice of the platform delivered at
sprint end. Under the \textbf{Definition of Done}, a requirement counts as done
only when (a)~the feature is implemented, (b)~at least one integration test covers
the happy path and one error path, and (c)~the corresponding capability has been
manually checked against its acceptance criteria.

\subsubsection{Sprint Structure}

Each sprint followed a fixed rhythm. \textbf{Sprint Planning} reviewed the backlog,
selected items, decomposed them into tasks, and estimated effort.
\textbf{Development} then implemented features, wrote integration tests, and
revieweded code against security and style guidelines. At \textbf{Sprint Review} the
working increment was demonstrated and checked against acceptance criteria. The
\textbf{Sprint Retrospective} closed the cycle by recording what worked, what
blocked progress, and what should change next time.

\subsubsection{Sprint Backlogs}

Tables~\ref{tab:s1-backlog}--\ref{tab:s5-backlog} detail each sprint backlog at
user-story level. Every story is linked to one or more requirements from
Table~\ref{tab:requirements} (or Table~\ref{tab:nfr} for non-functional items)
so that the expanded FR-01 to FR-36 catalogue remains traceable through planning,
design, and validation. Interaction flows for every story are documented in
Section~\ref{sec:sprint-design}.

Each story carries two planning attributes: a story-point estimate (\textbf{SP})
and a \textbf{priority}. Story points are a \emph{relative}, unitless measure of the
effort, complexity, and uncertainty of a story rather than a duration in hours; they
are estimated collaboratively (planning-poker style) on a Fibonacci-like scale
(2, 3, 5, 8, 13), where larger values indicate greater size or technical risk. This
relative scale makes estimates more consistent than absolute time predictions and,
once summed per sprint, gives an indication of the team's capacity (velocity).
Priority (\textbf{High} or \textbf{Medium} here) reflects how important and how
urgent a story is, and is chosen from three factors: business value for end users,
technical dependencies (stories that unblock later work are ranked higher), and
risk. Foundational stories such as authentication and access control are therefore
rated High, since subsequent sprints build directly upon them.
